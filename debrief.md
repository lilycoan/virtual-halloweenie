### Vocabulary

1. Prototyping and iteration (critical): A prototype is a working version used to explore or test an idea. Iteration uses what is learned from building and testing to revise the experience; repeated prompting without judging the result does not establish improvement.

2. Interactive storytelling, story beats, and pacing (critical): Interactive storytelling connects User action to an unfolding narrative. Story beats are meaningful moments or changes; pacing controls their timing and rhythm so the beginning, middle, and end can be understood within the story's duration.

3. Discoverability, affordance, and progressive hints (critical): Discoverability concerns whether Users can find what they can do. An affordance is an action made possible by an object or interface; visible or audible cues help communicate it. Progressive hints offer increasing guidance over time while preserving exploration.

4. Triggers and audiovisual feedback (critical): A trigger is an action or condition that starts a behavior. Feedback communicates how the system responded through visible and audible changes, including progress toward a story trigger. Feedback can occur before the complete trigger is satisfied.

5. State and state transitions (critical): State represents the relevant condition of the experience at a moment, such as trigger progress, active story, or paused status. A transition changes that condition according to an event or rule; the scene's behavior should consistently reflect it.

6. Interaction locking (critical): Temporarily preventing actions that would conflict with the current experience, such as starting another story during playback. The same rules must govern touch and keyboard, and release when appropriate. The Play/Pause control must remain usable while scene interactions are locked.

7. Audiovisual synchronization (critical): Coordinating sound, animation, and visual effects around the intended sequence and timing. Starting them together does not by itself establish that later story beats or resumed playback remain aligned.

8. Pause and resume (critical): Suspending the complete experience while preserving the information needed to continue it coherently. Motion, sound, effects, timers, and scene interaction must pause together; resuming should preserve intended progress and timing rather than silently restarting or skipping ahead.

9. Playtesting, observation, and evidence-driven revision (critical): Watching Users explore the experience, separating what they do or say from interpretations of why, and using that evidence to choose and check improvements. Explaining the triggers to a tester changes what can be learned about discovery.

10. Mobile accessibility and reduced motion (critical): Making the phone experience perceivable, understandable, and operable for people with different abilities, including readable text, contrast, suitable targets, and keyboard access. Reduced motion is a device preference to reduce or replace potentially uncomfortable motion; it must be considered before the experience begins and is distinct from manually pausing it.

11. Scope, build checkpoints, and living documents: Scope defines what the project includes and excludes. A checkpoint is a small working result that can be tested and judged. Living documents keep consequential evidence, intended behavior, and implementation progress current as the work develops, with each decision in its authoritative place.

12. Composition, focal point, and visual hierarchy: Composition arranges the scene's elements. A focal point draws attention, while visual hierarchy establishes their relative prominence and helps Users notice what matters. Visual emphasis should support the intended exploration on a phone screen.

13. Layering and transparency: Layering places scene elements in front of or behind one another. Transparency lets underlying content show through. Their use affects composition, effects, and whether visible interactive elements can actually receive input.

14. Assets, provenance, and licensing: Assets are resources such as images, sounds, and external code. Provenance records where they came from; licensing or public-domain status establishes permitted use. Credits acknowledge sources but do not substitute for permission or compliance with usage terms.

15. Ambience and soundscape: Ambience establishes the scene's ongoing atmosphere; a soundscape is its combined sound environment. Background loops and occasional sounds should support the setting while leaving story sounds and interaction feedback understandable.

16. Animation, keyframes, easing, and loops: Animation changes properties over time. Keyframes define values at selected moments, easing controls how change progresses between them, and a loop repeats a sequence. These choices shape mood, readability, and the transition between repetitions.

17. Events and timers: Events signal occurrences such as input or animation completion; timers schedule work after a delay or at intervals. Scheduled work must remain coordinated with the current state, including pause and replay, rather than firing into an outdated story or scene.

18. Reset, replay, and regression testing: Reset restores the intended starting conditions; replay allows a story to run again without reloading. Regression testing checks that a change has not broken previously working behavior, including repeated stories, hints, sound, and controls.

19. Browser autoplay restrictions: Browsers can block audible playback that begins without User interaction. The Enter action provides an opportunity to enable audio, but actual behavior must be checked on the target device; a start button alone does not prove that playback succeeded.

20. Deployment verification: Checking the publicly hosted version as Users receive it, including assets, sound, interaction, and phone layout. Local preview success does not establish that the intended version and all its resources work at the public HTTPS URL.

Reference guidance for explanations: [MDN's animation guidance](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Animations_API/Using_the_Web_Animations_API), [autoplay guidance](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Autoplay), [reduced-motion guidance](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion), and [W3C's accessibility introduction](https://www.w3.org/WAI/fundamentals/accessibility-intro/). These support explanations and are not additional Developer deliverables.
